# Transliteration: detect, then preserve

Hebrew has many valid English spellings. The speaker's pronunciation is part of their voice and often of their community, so the scribe's job is to detect the style and spell every restored word in it, never to impose one.

## The two families

**Ashkenazi** (yeshivish, Litvish, Chassidish, much of American Orthodoxy): final "s" where Sephardi has "t" (Shabbos, hilchos, kesubos, brachos, mitzvos, Malchus, Emes, Galus); "o" or "oi" vowels (Toirah in Chassidish, Moshe, Yankev, Shloime); "ch" for the guttural; "Rav", "Reb", "Rebbe"; sefer names as Mishnah Berurah, Shulchan Aruch, Krias Shema, Kesubos; Yiddish loanwords (davening, bentching, yontif, shul, gut Shabbos).

**Sephardi / Israeli / Mizrachi** (Sephardic communities, Israeli speakers, academic and Modern Orthodox usage): final "t" (Shabbat, hilchot, ketubot, berachot, mitzvot, Malchut, Emet, Galut); "a" vowels (Moshe, Yaakov, Shlomo); "ch", "kh" or "h" for the guttural (halacha, halakha, halaha; Chanukah, Hanukkah); "Rav", "Chacham", "Rabbi"; Keriat Shema, Ketubot, Birkat HaMazon, Shabbat shalom, chag sameach; Ladino or Arabic loanwords in Syrian, Moroccan, Iraqi communities (Chacham, Kabbalat Shabbat, Shabbat shalom u'mevorach).

**Mixed**: many speakers switch, especially when naming a sefer in its printed title ("Torat Kohanim", "Pirkei Avot") while otherwise speaking Ashkenazi, or when an Israeli speaker uses American yeshivish phrases. Preserve the switching; do not harmonize across the two systems.

## Detection

In the first few hundred words count markers: Shabbos, hilchos, brachos, mitzvos, Malchus, Emes, Galus, Krias, yontif, davening lean Ashkenazi; Shabbat, hilchot, berachot, mitzvot, Malchut, Emet, Galut, Keriat, chag, tefillah lean Sephardi. A clear majority sets the style for the whole transcript. A tie or an obvious mix means preserve each instance as heard.

Engines often spell the same word two ways because the engine, not the speaker, was inconsistent ("halacha" and "halakha" in one paragraph). That is the one case where you harmonize: pick the form that matches the detected style and use it throughout for that word.

## Applying the style to restored words

A restored word takes the speaker's style, not the dictionary's: an Ashkenazi speaker's garbled "nitz" becomes "netz" and "chatzos", a Sephardi speaker's becomes "netz" and "chatzot". Proper names of seforim keep their conventional spelling (Mishnah Berurah, Ben Ish Chai, Noda B'Yehuda, Igros Moshe) in both styles. Names of people keep the form the community uses (Rav Moshe Feinstein, Rabbi Ovadia Yosef, the Chazon Ish, Rashi).

## Pairs never to "correct" against each other

Shabbos / Shabbat; beracha / bracha / berakha; teshuva / tshuva / teshuvah; hilchos / hilchot / halakhot; Chazal / Hazal; chesed / hesed; Moshiach / Mashiach; Sh'ma / Shema; mikvah / mikveh; Chanukah / Hanukkah; Sukkos / Sukkot; Yisroel / Yisrael; Rosh Hashanah / Rosh Hashana; esrog / etrog; tzitzis / tzitzit; tallis / tallit; kesubah / ketubah; shul / beit knesset.

## Hebrew script in the transcript

Whisper-type engines sometimes emit a word in Hebrew letters inside an English sentence (for example אחד where the speaker said "echad"). Keep the meaning: transliterate it in the speaker's style and, when the speaker is quoting a source, keep the quote whole in transliteration. Do not delete it, and do not leave a lone Hebrew word where the rest of the quote is transliterated unless the whole transcript keeps Hebrew in Hebrew script.

## Sub-styles inside a family

Sephardi is a family, not one spelling. A Syrian speaker says Arbit, Harab, sedaka, Shabbat and kiddush; a Moroccan, Iraqi, Yemenite or Israeli speaker differs in the same slots (Arvit, HaRav, tzedakah). Ashkenazi has its own sub-styles (Litvish "Shabbos", Chassidish "Shabbes", Yekkish forms). Keep the sub-style you hear: do not normalize a Syrian "Arbit" to "Arvit" or "Harab" to "HaRav" because a reference or a printed sefer spells it so. The one legitimate harmonization is still the engine's own inconsistency: the same word spelled two ways in one transcript. When a quote is rebuilt from a printed source, keep the printed Hebrew wording but spell it the way this speaker pronounces it.
